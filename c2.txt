2. Literature Review
This chapter reviews literature across four thematic areas directly relevant to this project. Section 2.1 examines collaborative filtering architectures and the mechanisms through which demographic bias emerges within these systems. Section 2.2 surveys competing fairness definitions and the mathematical constraints governing their simultaneous satisfaction. Section 2.3 reviews the three categories of bias mitigation strategy evaluated in this study. Section 2.4 examines existing work on pipeline-level bias analysis and identifies the substantive gap that motivates the present research.

2.1 Collaborative Filtering and Bias Mechanisms
Collaborative filtering is grounded in the principle that “people who agreed in the past will probably agree again” (Resnick et al., 1994, p. 175). Each user’s agreement is measured by comparing rating preferences through similarity scores computed across a sparse user-item matrix where most entries are absent (Sarwar et al., 2001). Whilst this provides an effective predictive foundation, it implicitly treats historical interaction data as neutral. In practice, interaction data are shaped by exposure dynamics, popularity concentration, and unequal demographic participation rather than unbiased social behaviour (Ekstrand et al., 2018). If certain groups have historically engaged less with item categories due to limited exposure or structural barriers, collaborative filtering may learn to systematically under-recommend those categories regardless of actual preference, actively reinforcing and amplifying historical imbalances rather than merely reflecting them.
Matrix factorisation addressed scalability and sparsity limitations by decomposing user-item matrices into lower-dimensional latent factors that capture hidden preference structures without explicit similarity measures (Koren et al., 2009). Extensions including probabilistic matrix factorisation (Mnih & Salakhutdinov, 2008) and neural collaborative filtering (He et al., 2017) further improved modelling capacity. However, these architectures optimise primarily for accuracy metrics such as RMSE, so the model absorbs whatever demographic imbalances exist in the training data and carries them forward into its predictions. Optimising solely for predictive accuracy can therefore produce disproportionate recommendation quality across demographic groups even without explicit use of demographic attributes (Yao & Huang, 2017). Bias may enter through data imbalance, progressively amplify during similarity computation and neighbourhood formation, and persist through feedback loops that reinforce prior interaction patterns in subsequent model updates (Mansoury et al., 2020). User-based collaborative filtering was selected for this study despite these limitations because its transparent neighbourhood structure enables the stage-level decomposition required to trace bias propagation, a capability that latent factor models do not provide. This project looks at each of those stages where bias enters and amplifies, using a six-stage pipeline decomposition to identify where disparities emerge.

2.2 Fairness Definitions and Metrics
Fairness in recommender systems is not a single unified concept but a collection of competing definitions grounded in different normative assumptions (Ekstrand et al., 2022). Demographic parity requires equal recommendation rates across protected groups, such that |P(recommended | male) − P(recommended | female)| = 0. Equal opportunity requires equal true positive rates across groups, ensuring users with genuinely relevant items have equal retrieval chances regardless of group membership (Hardt et al., 2016). Calibration requires equivalent predictive accuracy, such as comparable RMSE values for male and female users (Chouldechova, 2017). Critically, these definitions may conflict: Chouldechova (2017) proves that perfect parity and calibration cannot be satisfied simultaneously unless base rates are identical across groups, meaning that in real-world datasets where demographic groups interact differently with items, achieving perfect fairness across both definitions simultaneously is unrealistic.
Optimising for predictive accuracy does not guarantee fairness, since high accuracy may reflect faithful reproduction of structural imbalances rather than equitable treatment (Yao & Huang, 2017). Corbett-Davies et al. (2017) argue this accuracy–fairness trade-off is a mathematical constraint arising when base rates differ between groups: algorithmic neutrality reproduces inequality rather than correcting it. Most existing fairness metrics are applied only at the final recommendation output, yet bias may accumulate during neighbourhood formation, similarity computation, or prediction generation. Ekstrand et al. (2018) analyse how demographic patterns propagate from user profiles to recommendations but focus on input–output relationships without a metric framework for intermediate stages, leaving a gap in understanding what happens at the intermediate pipeline stages.

2.3 Bias Mitigation Strategies
Mitigation strategies are categorised by the pipeline stage at which they intervene (Kamiran & Calders, 2012). Pre-processing approaches reduce demographic imbalances in training data before model fitting through resampling or reweighting. These methods are model-agnostic and straightforward to implement, though their simplicity limits depth of intervention. Simply duplicating female user ratings does not introduce new preference information; it only repeats existing interaction patterns without addressing the underlying demographic imbalance (Ekstrand et al., 2018). More sophisticated pre-processing alternatives such as data augmentation have been proposed to address this limitation, though they introduce additional complexity (Chen et al., 2023). In-processing approaches embed fairness constraints within the learning objective: Yao and Huang (2017) propose regularisation terms penalising rating disparities, though this approach assumes bias is primarily a prediction-level problem and does not address disparities that originate in training data or emerge during neighbourhood formation. Burke (2017) extends fairness obligations to item providers, highlighting that equitable treatment of content producers is frequently overlooked when fairness is framed solely around user outcomes. Kamishima et al. (2012) introduce a prejudice remover regulariser that reduces statistical dependence between predictions and sensitive attributes during training, though the method requires sensitive attribute information to be available at training time. These methods offer tighter integration but introduce accuracy trade-offs and are often algorithm-specific. Post-processing approaches adjust final outputs without modifying the model: Singh and Joachims (2018) frame re-ranking as a fairness of exposure problem, Zehlike et al. (2017) propose the FA*IR algorithm enforcing statistical parity at each rank position, and Steck (2018) examines calibration-based adjustment to align recommendations with user preference distributions. Post-processing is practically flexible but cannot address bias embedded in learned parameters. Each category therefore demonstrates partial effectiveness under specific conditions, motivating the comparative evaluation across all three stages of intervention adopted in this study (Jin et al., 2023).

2.4 Pipeline-Level Bias Analysis in Collaborative Filtering
Suresh and Guttag (2021) establish the theoretical foundation for treating bias as a pipeline-level phenomenon, though their taxonomy spans machine learning broadly and does not address the specific mechanisms through which bias propagates within recommender pipelines. Within recommender systems, Barocas and Selbst (2016) demonstrate that discriminatory outcomes arise from structural data properties without explicit design intent, implying that fairness interventions must target data and model structure rather than algorithmic intent alone. Abdollahpouri et al. (2019) show that popularity bias disproportionately disadvantages minority users underrepresented in dense interaction regions, a pattern directly observable in datasets where interaction density varies substantially across demographic groups. Mansoury et al. (2020) and Chaney et al. (2018) demonstrate that bias accumulates through feedback loops and algorithmic confounding respectively, yet neither study decomposes the pipeline into discrete stages for independent evaluation. Chen et al. (2020) and Mehrabi et al. (2021) survey bias taxonomies across machine learning systems but remain conceptual, offering no empirical evidence of how bias scores evolve across sequential algorithmic stages within collaborative filtering pipelines. More recent surveys confirm that fairness research continues to focus primarily on output-level measurement rather than pipeline-level decomposition (Deldjoo et al., 2023). This gap is significant: measuring bias only at the final output tells you that disparity exists but does not reveal the full story on how bias enters, amplifies, and where to intervene. The present study addresses this by implementing a six-stage decomposition framework covering input data, similarity computation, top similar user selection, neighbourhood formation, prediction generation, and final ranking, to empirically identify where bias enters, amplifies, and reduces across the recommendation process.

2.5 Summary
The literature establishes that collaborative filtering systems embed historical exposure imbalances before learning begins (Resnick et al., 1994; Ekstrand et al., 2018), that competing fairness definitions are not simultaneously satisfiable under realistic data conditions (Chouldechova, 2017; Hardt et al., 2016), and that pre-processing, in-processing, and post-processing interventions each carry stage-specific limitations (Kamiran & Calders, 2012; Yao & Huang, 2017; Singh & Joachims, 2018). A consistent gap emerges: fairness evaluation predominantly targets final outputs, treating the pipeline as a black box and hiding how bias enters the pipeline across intermediate stages. Whilst pipeline-level thinking is acknowledged theoretically (Suresh & Guttag, 2021; Chen et al., 2020), no existing work provides empirical stage-level fairness decomposition within collaborative filtering. The present study addresses this by measuring demographic bias independently across six pipeline stages and evaluating three mitigation strategies to determine whether stage-level intervention produces more equitable outcomes than output-level adjustment alone.
