5. Discussion and Conclusion

This chapter interprets the empirical findings reported in Chapter 4 in relation to the research questions motivating this study, the theoretical framework established in Chapter 2, and the broader context of fairness research in recommender systems. Section 5.1 discusses the pipeline decomposition findings and their implications for understanding where bias originates and amplifies. Section 5.2 examines the pre-processing oversampling result and its theoretical significance. Section 5.3 interprets the effectiveness and cost of the in-processing neighbourhood constraint. Section 5.4 discusses the post-processing null result and its implications for output-level fairness evaluation. Section 5.5 addresses the cross-strategy comparative findings and their implications for intervention design. Section 5.6 discusses the temporal stability findings. Section 5.7 acknowledges the limitations of the present study. Section 5.8 identifies directions for future research. Section 5.9 provides the conclusion.

5.1 The Pipeline as a Bias Amplifier: Interpreting the Stage-Level Decomposition

The central contribution of this study is the empirical demonstration that demographic bias does not distribute evenly across the collaborative filtering pipeline. The six-stage decomposition reveals a two-peak amplification profile in which bias rises sharply at Stage 2 similarity computation (from 0.180 to 0.309), partially corrects through the top-N selection filter at Stage 3 (falling to 0.042), re-amplifies at Stage 4 neighbourhood formation (rising to 0.083), and then reduces to a residual level at the output (0.027). This pattern has a direct and consequential implication: a fairness evaluation conducted exclusively at the final recommendation output — as the majority of existing work does — would measure a demographic parity gap of 0.027 and might reasonably conclude that the system is approaching equitable performance. The pipeline decomposition reveals that this conclusion would be structurally misleading, because the same system is simultaneously producing a peak internal disparity of 0.309 at Stage 2 — more than eleven times the output-level measurement.

This finding substantiates the theoretical gap identified in Section 2.4. Suresh and Guttag (2021) argue that harm in machine learning systems arises across multiple stages of the data and modelling pipeline, yet their taxonomy spans machine learning broadly and does not provide empirical stage-level measurements within recommender systems. Ekstrand et al. (2018) trace how demographic patterns propagate from user profiles to recommendations but focus on input–output relationships without a metric framework for intermediate stages. The present study fills this gap by providing empirical bias scores at each stage, identifying the bottleneck, and demonstrating that the bottleneck is neither the input data nor the final output but the similarity computation at Stage 2. This positions demographic homophily — the tendency of cosine similarity to cluster users along demographic lines — as the primary mechanism through which data-level imbalance is transformed into algorithmic disparity.

The mechanism underlying the Stage 2 bottleneck is consistent with prior theoretical accounts. Mansoury et al. (2020) show that bias amplification in recommender systems is not merely a consequence of biased training data but is actively generated by the algorithmic process itself. In the present study, male users contribute 75.4% of total ratings and interact with a wider item catalogue (3,671 unique items versus 3,481 for female users), producing denser and more overlapping rating vectors. Cosine similarity is sensitive to vector density and overlap: users with more ratings and broader item coverage exhibit higher mutual similarity scores simply because there are more co-rated items across which directional alignment can be measured. The result is that the similarity space reproduces the demographic imbalance in the training data at an amplified scale, with male–male pairs exhibiting systematically higher similarity scores than cross-gender or female–female pairs. This mechanism was not explicitly designed into the algorithm; it is an emergent property of applying cosine similarity to a demographically imbalanced input space, which is precisely what Barocas and Selbst (2016) mean when they argue that discriminatory outcomes arise from structural data properties without explicit design intent.

The secondary amplification at Stage 4 (neighbourhood composition gap of 0.083) reflects an additional structural dynamic. Even after the partial correction introduced by top-N selection at Stage 3, the KNN neighbourhood formation algorithm disproportionately selects male users into operational neighbourhoods because male users are more likely to have sufficient co-rated items to satisfy the minimum support threshold. Abdollahpouri et al. (2019) show that popularity bias disproportionately disadvantages minority users who are underrepresented in dense interaction regions; the Stage 4 finding is a direct expression of this mechanism within the neighbourhood formation process. Collectively, Stages 2 and 4 represent the two algorithmic points at which the system actively transforms data-level imbalance into distributional demographic disparity, and both operate well before the final recommendation output.

5.2 Why Pre-processing Oversampling Worsened Fairness

The finding that pre-processing oversampling worsened both the demographic parity gap (from 0.027 to 0.091) and the RMSE gap (from 0.024 to 0.090) is among the most significant results of this study. It is counterintuitive from a conventional fairness perspective — augmenting the representation of an underrepresented group in the training data is a standard recommendation in the fairness literature (Kamiran & Calders, 2012) — and its explanation lies precisely in the pipeline dynamics revealed by the Stage 2 analysis.

When female ratings are duplicated by a factor of two prior to training, the augmented training set contains a higher volume of female interactions. This increases the frequency with which female user rating vectors appear in the similarity computation, but it does not alter the structural density advantage of male users: male users still have broader item coverage and more diverse co-rated item sets, meaning their similarity scores remain higher in magnitude even after the augmentation. Crucially, by duplicating female ratings, the augmented data introduces repeated identical rating vectors for female users. Where two female users share a co-rated item, the oversampled versions of their ratings increase the apparent alignment between their vectors, concentrating female similarity scores within a narrower demographic cluster. The consequence is that the similarity space becomes more, not less, demographically segregated: male similarity scores and female similarity scores become more concentrated within their respective groups, widening the cross-group disparity that drives the Stage 2 bottleneck.

This explanation is consistent with Ekstrand et al.'s (2018) observation that "simply duplicating female ratings does not introduce new preference information" and may distort similarity computation at subsequent stages. The present study provides direct empirical evidence of this distortion: oversampling improved aggregate predictive accuracy (overall RMSE 0.974 → 0.939) and ranking quality (NDCG@10 0.924 → 0.939) because the additional female rating vectors gave the model more signal to optimise its predictions across the full user population. However, the distribution of that accuracy improvement was unequal, with the demographic parity and RMSE gaps widening substantially. This is consistent with the accuracy–fairness tension described by Corbett-Davies et al. (2017): when base rates differ between demographic groups, interventions that improve aggregate accuracy can simultaneously worsen between-group equity, because they are faithfully representing a training distribution that is itself inequitable.

The practical implication is significant. An evaluation framework that monitors only overall RMSE and NDCG would record the oversampling intervention as unambiguously beneficial: accuracy improved on both measures. Only the pipeline-level decomposition and group-level fairness metrics expose the simultaneous deterioration in demographic equity. This illustrates why Deldjoo et al. (2023) argue that fairness evaluation in recommender systems requires metrics beyond aggregate performance, and reinforces the case for stage-level monitoring as a standard component of bias auditing rather than an optional complement.

5.3 In-processing Neighbourhood Constraint: Effectiveness and Trade-offs

The in-processing neighbourhood constraint produced the largest fairness improvements of the three strategies evaluated: the demographic parity gap reduced by 37% (from 0.027 to 0.017), the RMSE gap reduced by 79% (from 0.024 to 0.005), and the neighbourhood composition gap reduced by 79% (from 0.083 to 0.017). These improvements are mechanistically coherent with the pipeline decomposition findings: because Stage 4 neighbourhood formation is the second amplification point in the pipeline, a constraint that directly regulates neighbourhood demographic composition disrupts the re-amplification mechanism and propagates a fairness benefit forward into Stage 5 prediction generation and Stage 6 ranking. This confirms that stage-targeted intervention, informed by the decomposition framework, produces more effective outcomes than stage-agnostic strategies.

The cost of this improvement — an overall RMSE increase from 0.974 to 1.187 (+21.9%) — is substantial and requires contextualisation. The accuracy deterioration is a direct and predictable consequence of the constraint mechanism: enforcing a minimum female representation of 30% in each neighbourhood requires, in some cases, including female neighbours whose cosine similarity to the target user is lower than the displaced male neighbours. The constraint therefore introduces a systematic trade-off between representational fairness and similarity-based prediction precision. This is precisely the mathematical relationship formalised by Corbett-Davies et al. (2017), who demonstrate that parity constraints introduce accuracy penalties whenever group base rates differ — which in this dataset they clearly do, with male users contributing 71.7% of total interactions. The 21.9% RMSE increase is therefore not a failure of the implementation but a direct quantification of the accuracy cost that demographic parity, in this dataset and model configuration, necessarily entails.

Importantly, NDCG@10 remained unchanged at 0.924 following the neighbourhood constraint, indicating that whilst prediction accuracy was affected, the quality of item ranking within recommendation lists was preserved. This distinction matters for practical deployment: a system where predictions are slightly less accurate but recommendation lists are equivalently well-ordered may be an acceptable operational trade-off in contexts where demographic equity is a primary design objective. Burke (2017) argues that fairness obligations in recommender systems extend to multiple stakeholders, including item providers; the in-processing result demonstrates that a fairness gain for one user group (female users receiving more equitable neighbourhood representation) can be achieved without degrading the discovery and ranking experience for either group, even at a cost to raw predictive precision.

The constraint threshold of 30% minimum female representation was selected as a conservative target below the actual female population proportion (28.3%), intended to produce a measurable effect without maximally disrupting similarity-based selection. The fact that this threshold produced both the fairest outcome and the largest accuracy cost among the strategies tested suggests that the constraint is binding — it is genuinely redistributing neighbourhood composition rather than merely formalising an existing demographic balance. A lower threshold might preserve more accuracy at reduced fairness benefit; a higher threshold would extend the fairness benefit at greater accuracy cost. This parameter represents an explicit, adjustable control over the accuracy–fairness trade-off, which is a more transparent and principled approach to bias management than strategies that produce unintended fairness effects as a side consequence of accuracy optimisation.

5.4 Why Post-processing Re-ranking Had No Effect

The complete absence of any fairness improvement from the post-processing re-ranking strategy — with the demographic parity gap and RMSE gap both unchanged at 0.027 and 0.024 respectively, and a small NDCG@10 cost of 0.005 — is a theoretically important null result. It directly addresses a persistent assumption in the fairness literature: that output-level adjustments can compensate for disparities embedded earlier in the pipeline.

The decomposition framework explains why this assumption fails in the present case. The demographic parity gap at Stage 6 is a residual of bias dynamics that originate at Stage 2 (similarity computation) and re-amplify at Stage 4 (neighbourhood formation). These dynamics are encoded in the predicted rating values themselves: when the model has been trained on an imbalanced similarity space and has formed demographically skewed neighbourhoods, the resulting predictions already reflect those structural inequities. Re-ranking the final list redistributes item presentation but does not alter the predicted ratings from which the list was constructed. The gap therefore persists because its source is in the model's internal representations, not in the final output format.

This finding is consistent with Singh and Joachims' (2018) argument that fairness of exposure in rankings is a meaningful objective, but that its practical impact is constrained when the recommendations themselves are the product of an inequitable prediction process. Zehlike et al.'s (2017) FA*IR algorithm and similar post-processing approaches are designed to enforce statistical parity at each rank position, but their effectiveness depends on there being sufficient latitude in item selection to reorder without disrupting list integrity. In the present experiment, the re-ranking altered item positions without changing which items were included, because the predicted rating scores — which determine list composition — remained unchanged. A re-ranking approach that simultaneously altered which items were eligible for inclusion (for example, by adjusting the candidate pool based on fairness criteria) might produce a different result, but this would represent a more invasive intervention that approaches the boundary between post-processing and in-processing.

The null result reinforces the core methodological argument of this study: fairness interventions cannot be selected on the basis of operational convenience alone. Post-processing is attractive because it requires no modification of the model or training pipeline, making it easy to deploy and reverse. But its effectiveness is contingent on the bias being located at the output stage, and the decomposition framework reveals that in this pipeline the output stage is not where the bias resides. Selecting an intervention without first diagnosing the pipeline stage at which amplification occurs risks investing in measures that produce no fairness benefit, and possibly — as the pre-processing result demonstrates — that actively worsen the outcome.

5.5 Implications for Fairness Intervention Design

Taken together, the three mitigation results produce a coherent and generalisable principle: the effectiveness of a fairness intervention in a collaborative filtering pipeline is determined by its alignment with the stage at which bias amplification occurs, not by the category of intervention selected. In-processing succeeded because it targeted Stage 4, which the decomposition identified as an active amplification point. Pre-processing failed on fairness grounds because it targeted Stage 1 without addressing the Stage 2 mechanism that converts input-level imbalance into algorithmic disparity. Post-processing had no effect because it targeted Stage 6, downstream of both amplification points.

This principle has direct implications for how fairness auditing and intervention selection should be approached in practice. The conventional categorisation of mitigation strategies into pre-, in-, and post-processing (Kamiran & Calders, 2012) describes where in the pipeline an intervention acts, but does not specify whether that location corresponds to a genuine amplification point in the specific system being evaluated. The pipeline decomposition framework provides the diagnostic tool that connects these two questions: it identifies where amplification occurs, enabling practitioners to select the intervention category whose operational stage matches the amplification stage rather than defaulting to whichever strategy is most convenient to implement.

More broadly, the finding that output-level measurement systematically understates peak internal disparity has implications beyond this study. Deldjoo et al. (2023) confirm that fairness research continues to focus predominantly on output-level measurement; the present results demonstrate the cost of this focus. A system that registers a DP gap of 0.027 at Stage 6 but a peak disparity of 0.309 at Stage 2 is not a nearly fair system with a small residual problem — it is a system in which demographic homophily is actively shaping the entire recommendation process, and the output-level measurement is simply the net residual after several partial corrections have occurred. Treating this residual as the primary fairness signal would lead to systematically underinvesting in bias mitigation and to selecting interventions (such as post-processing) that address the residual rather than the source.

5.6 Temporal Stability and Structural Bias

The temporal stability analysis demonstrates that the bias profile identified in the full-dataset experiments is not specific to a particular interaction period but persists consistently across four sequential windows. Stage 2 remains the dominant amplification stage in every window, with bias scores ranging from 0.324 to 0.405. The input-level activity gap (Stage 1) fluctuates narrowly between 0.167 and 0.183, indicating that the demographic imbalance in interaction volume is a stable structural property rather than a transient condition.

The absence of statistically significant temporal trends (all p > 0.57) indicates that no stage's bias score is systematically increasing or decreasing across the observation period. The Stage 4 slope (0.006, p = 0.577) hints at a marginal tendency for neighbourhood composition bias to increase over time, which would be consistent with the feedback loop dynamics described by Mansoury et al. (2020) and Chaney et al. (2018): as the system repeatedly recommends from a demographically skewed neighbourhood, and users interact with those recommendations, the subsequent interaction data reinforces the existing similarity structure, potentially widening the neighbourhood composition gap incrementally. However, this trend is not statistically significant within the four-window sample and should not be treated as an established finding. Its presence as a directional signal nonetheless suggests that longitudinal monitoring of Stage 4 bias is warranted in any production deployment of a similar system.

The stability finding carries a reassuring implication for the generalisability of the mitigation results: because the bias structure does not change substantially across time windows, interventions calibrated on the full dataset can be expected to encounter the same amplification dynamics in any temporal segment of the data. It also carries a sobering implication: the structural nature of the bias means it will not self-correct over time. Without active intervention at the amplification stages identified by the decomposition, the demographic disparities observed in this study will persist indefinitely.

5.7 Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, gender is represented as a binary attribute in the MovieLens 1M dataset and is self-reported, meaning it may not fully reflect the diversity of user identities. The fairness analysis is therefore specific to this binary representation and cannot be generalised to non-binary or more granular demographic structures without methodological extension. All references to gender-based disparity in this study are conditional on this representation constraint.

Second, the dataset was collected between 2000 and 2003, and the interaction patterns it contains reflect the platform context and media consumption habits of that period. Whilst the temporal stability analysis confirms internal consistency across the four windows within this dataset, it cannot confirm whether the same bias dynamics would manifest in a contemporary streaming platform with different item catalogues, user demographics, and interaction modalities.

Third, the pre-processing strategy evaluated in this study employed simple random oversampling — the most straightforward implementation within its category. More sophisticated pre-processing approaches, such as the data augmentation method proposed by Chen et al. (2023), which generates synthetic interaction data rather than duplicating existing ratings, were not evaluated. It is possible that augmentation-based pre-processing would avoid the distortion effect observed with oversampling by introducing new preference information rather than repeating existing patterns. The negative result reported for pre-processing in Section 4.3.1 should therefore be interpreted as specific to oversampling rather than as a categorical indictment of all pre-processing approaches.

Fourth, the model configuration was held constant throughout all experiments, with k=50 and cosine similarity fixed parameters. No sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess whether the bias profile or the relative effectiveness of mitigation strategies changes under different neighbourhood sizes or similarity measures. The bottleneck identification at Stage 2 is specific to cosine similarity; a model using Pearson correlation or a weighted similarity measure might produce a different inter-stage bias profile.

Fifth, the study evaluates a single dataset. Whilst MovieLens 1M is a widely used benchmark that enables comparison with existing literature, the demographic imbalance (71.7% male versus 28.3% female) is specific to this dataset, and the magnitude of the observed disparities may differ in datasets with more balanced demographic distributions. The qualitative pattern of bottleneck stages may nonetheless generalise, but this requires empirical validation across additional datasets.

5.8 Future Research Directions

Several directions for future research emerge from the findings and limitations of this study.

The six-stage decomposition framework was designed specifically for user-based collaborative filtering, where the pipeline stages are transparent and independently extractable. Applying an analogous decomposition to matrix factorisation or neural collaborative filtering models would require different stage definitions, as latent factor representations do not produce explicit similarity matrices or neighbourhood lists. Developing stage-level fairness metrics for latent factor architectures is a technically challenging but important extension, given that these models dominate production recommender systems (Koren et al., 2009; He et al., 2017).

The negative pre-processing result motivates evaluation of more sophisticated data augmentation approaches. Chen et al. (2023) propose augmentation strategies that generate synthetic interaction data conditioned on demographic group membership, introducing genuinely new preference signals rather than duplicating existing ones. Testing whether these approaches avoid the similarity distortion observed with oversampling — whilst retaining the accuracy benefits — would provide a more complete picture of pre-processing viability.

The present study evaluates fairness along a single demographic dimension (gender). Extending the decomposition framework to simultaneously measure bias along age, occupation, or intersectional demographic categories would reflect the multi-dimensional nature of real-world user diversity and may reveal interactions between demographic variables that are not visible in single-dimension analysis.

The finding that in-processing neighbourhood constraint reduces fairness gaps most effectively at a substantial accuracy cost suggests that hybrid strategies merit investigation. A pipeline in which oversampling is applied at Stage 1 only to the extent that it does not widen the Stage 2 similarity gap — informed by real-time monitoring of the decomposition — could potentially provide input-level demographic balance without triggering the amplification effect observed in the present study. Combining this with a moderate neighbourhood constraint at Stage 4 and a calibrated post-processing step targeted at improving recall for female users rather than parity at the output level might reduce the accuracy–fairness trade-off relative to any single strategy applied in isolation.

Finally, the hint of a positive temporal trend in Stage 4 neighbourhood composition bias motivates longitudinal study of feedback loop dynamics over extended interaction periods. A simulation framework in which recommendations generated by the system are fed back as new training interactions, following the design of Mansoury et al. (2020) and Chaney et al. (2018), would enable direct empirical testing of whether Stage 4 bias compounds over recommendation cycles in the way the temporal slope suggests.

5.9 Conclusion

This study set out to address a substantive gap in the fairness literature: whilst pipeline-level thinking about bias has been acknowledged theoretically (Suresh & Guttag, 2021; Chen et al., 2020), no prior work had provided empirical stage-level fairness decomposition within a collaborative filtering pipeline. The present research addressed this gap by implementing a six-stage decomposition framework on the MovieLens 1M dataset and evaluating three mitigation strategies — pre-processing oversampling, in-processing neighbourhood constraint, and post-processing re-ranking — against both fairness and accuracy metrics at each stage.

Four findings emerge from this work. First, demographic bias in this collaborative filtering pipeline is not uniform across stages: it amplifies sharply at Stage 2 similarity computation (from 0.180 to 0.309), representing an eleven-fold amplification of the output-level measurement, and re-amplifies at Stage 4 neighbourhood formation (0.083) before reducing to a residual level at the final output (0.027). Measuring fairness only at the output therefore provides a systematically incomplete — and potentially misleading — account of the system's demographic equity.

Second, pre-processing oversampling worsened both fairness metrics whilst improving aggregate accuracy, confirming and extending Ekstrand et al.'s (2018) theoretical warning that simple rating duplication cannot address imbalances embedded in the similarity computation stage, and demonstrating that output-only evaluation frameworks would have obscured this deterioration by recording only the accuracy improvement.

Third, in-processing neighbourhood constraint produced the largest fairness improvements — reducing the RMSE gap by 79% and the demographic parity gap by 37% — by directly targeting the Stage 4 amplification point identified in the decomposition. The associated accuracy cost (RMSE +21.9%) is a quantifiable and principled expression of the accuracy–fairness trade-off that is mathematically unavoidable when group base rates differ, and its magnitude can be controlled through adjustment of the constraint threshold.

Fourth, post-processing re-ranking had no measurable effect on any fairness metric, because the bias it attempted to correct was structurally embedded in the similarity space and neighbourhood composition, not in the final output format. This null result empirically demonstrates that output-level intervention is insufficient when the causal source of disparity lies at intermediate pipeline stages.

Taken together, these findings make the case that stage-level bias decomposition should be a standard component of fairness auditing for recommender systems rather than an optional supplement to output-level measurement. As fairness obligations are increasingly codified in regulation — including GDPR Article 22's right to explanation and the EU AI Act's (2024) transparency requirements — the ability to identify not merely that a system produces inequitable outcomes but precisely where in the pipeline those outcomes are generated will become an operational necessity rather than a research aspiration. The six-stage decomposition framework developed in this study offers a replicable, interpretable, and practically actionable approach to meeting that need.
